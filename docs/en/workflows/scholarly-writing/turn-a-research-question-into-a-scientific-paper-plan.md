# How do I turn a research question into a scientific paper plan?

<div class="answer-meta" markdown>
<span>Scholarly writing</span><span>beginner</span><span>75–120 min</span>
</div>

## What you are trying to do

How can a broad interest become an argument that a particular scholarly audience can evaluate? A scientific or scholarly paper does more than report a topic. It poses a researchable question, offers a provisional answer, presents evidence, explains the reasoning, addresses alternatives, and states limits.

Humanities genres vary. A textual interpretation, historical case study, theoretical essay, edition commentary, and digital-method report do not share one mandatory architecture. IMRaD may be appropriate for an empirical study, while another paper may move through a problem, archive, interpretive sequence, and implications. Do not impose IMRaD or a five-paragraph essay as a universal template.

## You need

- the assignment or publication brief, intended audience, length, and citation requirements;
- one bounded research question and three to five checked sources;
- reading notes that separate quotations, paraphrases, summaries, and your own response;
- the [sample ZIP](../../../assets/downloads/scholarly-work-foundations-v1.zip), which includes reading notes, a one-page plan, source-to-claim table, revised paragraph, and known patchwriting problem.

## Workflow

1. **Name the audience and genre.** Write one sentence: `For [readers], this [genre] investigates [problem] in order to clarify [stake].` Inspect two credible examples from the discipline. Record how they handle evidence and sections without copying their titles as a universal outline.

2. **Tighten the question.** Replace a topic such as “digitised postcards” with a question that names material, relation, scope, and uncertainty: `How do inconsistent catalogue descriptions affect which creators can be found in a small digitised postcard collection?` A good question permits more than one defensible answer.

3. **Write a provisional claim.** Answer in one or two sentences. Make the claim arguable, bounded, and revisable. Then write the strongest reasonable alternative answer. If no informed reader could disagree, the statement may be a description rather than a thesis.

4. **List evidence, counterexample, and limitation.** For every supporting source or observation, state what it shows and what reasoning connects it to the claim. Add one counterexample or rival interpretation. Add one limitation of the material, model, scale, or method. A limitation narrows the inference; it need not invalidate the project.

5. **Build a source-to-claim table.** Use one row per planned claim. Include source, exact locator, evidence or observation, interpretive use, possible objection, and verification status. A bibliography tells readers what you consulted; this table tells you what argumentative work each source can support.

6. **Map sections as moves.** For each section, write a question it answers, a local claim, its evidence, the transition from the previous section, and the reason the next section is needed. A section title should describe a role in the argument rather than a file of miscellaneous notes.

7. **Plan paragraphs as reasoning.** Draft a sequence: **claim → evidence → reasoning → transition**. The pattern is diagnostic, not a demand that every paragraph contain exactly four sentences. A quotation without your reasoning is an orphan; a claim without inspectable support is unsupported; a transition should state a conceptual relation rather than merely say “next.”

8. **Distinguish three uses of sources.** A **quotation** reproduces exact words and requires precise transcription, quotation marks or block treatment, and a locator. A **paraphrase** restates a specific passage substantially in your own syntax and emphasis and still needs a citation. A **summary** compresses a larger argument or body of material and names its scope. None is automatically more scholarly than the others.

9. **Write from closed notes, then verify.** Record a passage and locator, close or hide the source, explain the idea for your present question, and then reopen the source to check accuracy and wording. If the draft follows the source sentence by sentence with substituted synonyms, revise it: patchwriting obscures whose language and reasoning are operating.

10. **Draft and revise one sample paragraph.** Mark its claim, evidence, reasoning, and transition. Verify the quotation or paraphrase against the source. Add the counterexample where it genuinely changes the reasoning rather than in a token final sentence.

11. **Write the abstract last.** After the argument is stable, write a short abstract naming the problem, material or corpus, approach, main claim or finding, and most important implication or limit. Do not promise evidence or conclusions that the paper no longer contains.

## Output

Produce a one-page argument plan, a source-to-claim table, and one revised sample paragraph. The plan should include audience, genre, question, provisional claim, alternative answer, section moves, evidence, counterexample, and limitation.

The workflow passes when every section advances the central question, every planned evidentiary claim has a source and locator, and the revised paragraph visibly connects evidence to reasoning without patchwriting.

## Check yourself

- Would a reasonable reader understand why the question matters and be able to disagree with the claim?
- Does each source support the use assigned to it rather than merely share a topic?
- Is evidence distinguished from your interpretation of it?
- Does the counterexample change, narrow, or test the claim?
- Does the abstract describe the finished argument rather than the initial intention?

## Common traps

- Treating a topic, research question, and thesis as synonyms.
- Using section headings as containers instead of argumentative moves.
- Inserting a quotation and expecting it to interpret itself.
- Paraphrasing by replacing words while keeping the source's syntax.
- Forcing every humanities paper into IMRaD or five paragraphs.
- Writing an abstract early and never reconciling it with the final paper.

## Practice task

Use the sample notes to produce two different section maps for the same question. Choose one, explain what the other would foreground, and revise the known-problem paragraph so its source use, reasoning, and limitation are visible.
